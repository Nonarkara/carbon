About this project · How it works

Weighing Thailand's forests from space, with open data anyone can check

Tap a province and three numbers appear: how much carbon the forest holds, how much it takes in each year, and how much is released. This page explains, step by step, where those numbers come from, and where they stop being trustworthy.

01 Three questions, not one

"How much carbon is in this forest?" sounds like one question. It is really three, and each needs different evidence.

02 How a satellite weighs a forest

No satellite can put a tree on a scale. Radar satellites, including JAXA's ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 and Europe's Sentinel-1, send microwaves down and listen to the echo. Tall, dense forest returns a different echo from grass or rice. Scientists at the European Space Agency's Climate Change Initiative turned those echoes into weight with models calibrated on forest data, and published a map of aboveground biomass, the dry weight of trunks, branches and leaves, for every 100 m square on Earth. Each square also comes with its own error estimate.

To decide which part of each square is forest, the map uses JAXA's own forest / non-forest map for the same year, made from the same kind of radar. It calls of Thailand forest. The Royal Forest Department counts 31.64% for 2020, because radar sees any tree canopy, including rubber and fruit orchards. That difference is a reminder that "forest" always depends on who is defining it.

03 From a tonne of wood to tonnes of CO₂

Satellites measure wood above the ground. Carbon accounting needs CO₂. Three conversions get there, the same ones Thailand's T-VER tree tool uses for general trees.

04 Count every square once

A number that changes depending on how you add it up is not a number you can trust. So the system follows one rule. Every 100 m square belongs to exactly one province, decided by where its centre falls, and to exactly one 2.8 km cell of the drawing grid. Add up all the provinces, all the cells, or the whole country, and you get the same total. The software checks this every time it is built.

05 Two sides of the ledger

For all of Thailand, Global Forest Watch's model estimates how much CO₂ forests took up and released each year on average from 2001 to 2025. Beside those figures the map shows two other kinds of emission from the same land: all fires, and fossil fuels. They are drawn on one scale so you can compare sizes. They are never added up, because each comes from a different dataset, period and method, and some overlap.

06 The fire calendar

Satellites that detect burned ground show a clear rhythm. Most fire carbon in Thailand is released between February and April, when northern forests and fields burn in the dry season. That is the same season the haze arrives.

07 Smoke is not carbon dioxide

Haze maps are easy to find, so it is tempting to read them as carbon maps. They measure different things.

08 Honest about uncertainty

Every 100 m square has an error estimate. How those errors add up across a province depends on whether neighbouring squares tend to be wrong in the same direction, and nobody knows that exactly. So the map shows two ranges and lets you see the gap between them.

09 The open-data recipe

Everything here is built from data published openly by space agencies, research groups and governments. Anyone can repeat it.

10 What this cannot tell you

  • It is not a carbon credit. Credits need a baseline, proof of additional benefit, permanence, leakage checks and independent verification. For project work, use the T-VER workbench tabs, which rely on your own field measurements.
  • It is not today. Stock is for 2020, forest flux is a 2001–2025 average, fire is 2013–2022, and fossil CO₂ is 2024.
  • It is not a parcel survey. Drawn boxes work at about 2.8 km. A single farm or community forest needs field plots.
  • It is not approved. TGO must approve any remote-sensing method used for T-VER credits. These products have no such approval.
